The signal your competitors can’t buy

Bought intent data tells you who to call. The signal already sitting in your CRM, your inbox and your call recordings tells you why and when — and no competitor can purchase a copy of it.

In one paragraph

The summary

Cody Leovic runs growth for EMEA at Verkada — a B2B physical security company past $1B in annual revenue, roughly a quarter of it from marketing-sourced leads. His argument: teams pour budget into third-party intent data that every competitor can buy, while ignoring the signal their buyers hand over for free. Purchased signals saturate. Everyone pulls the same list, everyone sends the same “personalised” email, and the edge evaporates the moment the arbitrage window closes.

What compounds instead is an owned data layer: CRM notes, past email correspondence, call transcripts, product usage, competitor mentions in lost deals. Verkada mines those into signals and routes them back into Salesforce, so reps act on what buyers already told them. The clearest proof is the least glamorous one — a re-engagement sequence built purely on buyer-stated timing replies at about 33%, with roughly half of those going to demo.

The problem

A rented signal is an arbitrage window, not a moat

Third-party data can be entirely accurate and still stop working. Its value comes from being early, not from being true. The vendor sells the same feed to your competitors; every buyer of that feed builds roughly the same play; the target receives five variations of the same “I saw you’re hiring for X” email in a week. Accuracy survives, advantage doesn’t.

First-party signal moves the other way. It is generated by your own buyer interactions, it accumulates every time someone replies or takes a call, and it is structurally unavailable to anyone else.

The distinction

Two kinds of signal, two different jobs

This is the line to hold onto. Third-party data is a coverage tool — it populates the list. First-party signal is a message-and-timing tool — it decides what you say and when you say it. Neither replaces the other; confusing them is what produces high-volume, low-reply outbound.

The inventory

Five signal sources you already own

None of these require a new vendor. The work is extraction and routing, not acquisition — which is why most teams underuse them: buying a list is a purchase order, mining your own systems is a project.

CRM notes

Free-text context a rep already recorded: budget cycle, incumbent vendor, the objection that stalled the deal.

Use it for: reviving stalled opportunities with the objection already answered.

Email replies

Every human answer — including the ones filed as rejections — carries fit and timing information.

Use it for: re-engagement timed to what the buyer actually said.

Call transcripts

Verkada mines Gong transcripts for buying signals: stated pain, named competitors, decision timelines.

Use it for: message extraction at scale, and account prioritisation.

Product usage

Which accounts are expanding, which features they lean on, where adoption has plateaued.

Use it for: expansion plays and churn-risk flags before renewal.

Lost-deal intelligence

Who you lost to and why — competitor intelligence you generated yourself, deal by deal.

Use it for: displacement plays timed to the incumbent’s renewal.

Worked example

The reply most reps file as a loss

This is the whole thesis compressed into one email. The buyer volunteers two separate pieces of information; the average pipeline treats it as one, and throws both away.

What arrives

“Your message resonates, but I’m on holiday. Reach out in a few months.”

Marked closed — not interested by most reps.

What it actually contains

Fit confirmed. “Your message resonates” is qualification the buyer performed for you, for free.

Timing stated. The buyer named the window. You no longer have to guess it.

What Verkada does with it

Captures the stated date as a first-party signal, holds the account, and fires a re-engagement sequence when the buyer’s own window opens — referencing the earlier exchange.

Enriched context flows back into Salesforce so the rep sees it in the workflow.

Result

~33% reply rate on that sequence, with roughly half converting to demos. Outbound reply rate doubled overall after the workflow went live.

The takeaways

Eight things worth remembering

Ordered by how much each one changes what you do next week, not by where they appear in the article.

01

A bought signal commoditises on contact

What you pay for is a head start, not an advantage. The moment a competitor writes the same cheque, the edge is gone — while the data stays perfectly accurate.

ApplyAsk of every purchased signal: what happens to this play when three competitors run it too?

02

Owned signal compounds

Every call, reply and note adds to a layer nobody else can query. A purchased list decays from the day you buy it; an owned layer is worth more each quarter you keep feeding it.

ApplyTreat capture as infrastructure, not admin. Untracked conversations are lost assets.

03

Who versus why and when

Third-party data tells you who to call. First-party signal tells you why and when. Use bought data for coverage and owned data for the message and the moment.

ApplyAudit your last 20 outbound emails: how many drew their opening line from something the buyer told you?

04

“Not now” is a qualified yes with a timestamp

A reply that validates the message but defers the timing is two signals, not a rejection. Filing it as closed-lost destroys the highest-quality data in the funnel.

ApplyAdd a disposition for “fit confirmed, timing deferred” and capture the date the buyer named.

05

Buyer-stated timing outperforms inferred intent

The 33% reply rate is not the product of better copy. It works because the buyer chose the moment and you simply honoured it.

ApplyBuild one re-engagement sequence sourced entirely from timing the buyer stated in writing.

06

The signal is already in your systems

Email history, transcripts, CRM notes, product usage, lost-deal notes. Nothing new gets purchased — the constraint is extraction and structure, which is why it stays undone.

ApplyPick the single richest source you own and extract one signal from it end to end.

07

Capture without routing is just storage

Verkada pushes enriched intelligence back into Salesforce. A signal that never reaches the rep inside their normal workflow changes no behaviour and produces no pipeline.

ApplyFor every signal you build, name the field, the view and the moment a human sees it.

08

Marketing-sourced pipeline is a systems output

Around a quarter of a $1B+ business coming from marketing-sourced leads is not a copywriting result. It is what operationalised owned signal looks like at scale.

ApplyJudge the programme on routed signals in production, not on campaigns shipped.

Check yourself

Six questions before you close the page

Answer each one out loud first, then open it. Recall is what makes this stick — rereading the cards above will not.

Why does a third-party intent signal lose value even when the data stays accurate?

Because its value came from scarcity, not truth. The vendor sells the same feed to everyone; once competitors run the same play against the same accounts, the buyer sees near-identical outreach and the edge closes. Accuracy is unchanged; advantage is gone.

What different question does each type of signal answer?

Third-party answers who to call — it builds coverage. First-party answers why and when — it decides the message and the moment.

A prospect replies “interesting, but try me in Q3.” What are the two distinct pieces of information?

One: fit is confirmed — the buyer just qualified themselves. Two: timing is stated — you no longer have to infer the window. Filing it as a rejection throws away both.

Name the owned signal sources any established GTM team already has.

CRM notes, past email correspondence and replies, call transcripts (Gong or equivalent), product usage data, and competitor intelligence from lost deals.

Why does routing matter as much as capture?

A signal only changes behaviour if a rep encounters it inside their existing workflow. Verkada writes enriched intelligence back into Salesforce for exactly this reason — a signal sitting in a warehouse is storage, not go-to-market.

What breaks first if you scale this without discipline?

Input quality. Free-text CRM notes and un-dispositioned replies are inconsistent, so extraction quality caps the entire system. The moat is only as good as the hygiene of what feeds it — and unlike a purchased list, you cannot buy a replacement.

Practice

A five-day drill on your own data

Understanding this takes an afternoon; internalising it takes one pass through your own systems. Small scope on purpose — one signal, end to end.

  1. Pull every reply from the last two quarters that deferred on timing rather than rejecting. Count them. The number is usually uncomfortable.
  2. Extract the stated timing from each one and record it as a structured field — a date, not a note.
  3. Write the re-engagement message. One rule: it must open by referencing what that specific buyer said, and nothing else.
  4. Route it — a CRM view, a task, an alert. Name the exact moment a human will see the signal.
  5. Fire the first cohort whose window has opened and record the reply rate. Compare it with your standard outbound baseline.

Vocabulary

Terms used precisely

First-party signal
Behavioural or stated information generated by your own interactions with a buyer — replies, calls, notes, product usage. Not purchasable by anyone else.
Third-party intent
Buying-interest data licensed from a vendor: research activity, hiring, technographics. Available to every competitor holding the same contract.
Arbitrage window
The period during which a bought signal still produces above-market results, before enough competitors adopt it to saturate the target’s inbox.
Signal decay
The loss of a signal’s effectiveness over time — through saturation, or because the underlying event goes stale.
Signal routing
Delivering a detected signal into the system where a rep already works, with enough context to act without leaving it.
Marketing-sourced revenue
Revenue traced to opportunities marketing originated rather than expanded or influenced — roughly a quarter of Verkada’s total.
